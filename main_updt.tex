\begin{abstract}
Anticipating the impacts of a future large earthquake is essential for effective humanitarian contingency planning. Ideally, that planning should incorporate both the range of potential future earthquake locations and magnitudes, and the full range of earthquake-related hazards - which in many continental settings includes coseismic landsliding. Here, we extend a previously-developed earthquake scenario ensemble approach to explicitly include coseismic landsliding, using a hypergraph network model to efficiently quantify multi-hazard risk across the ensemble. We apply this model to a set of 30 plausible large-magnitude ($M_w > 7$) earthquakes across Nepal to evaluate the combined impacts of shaking and landsliding on building and road infrastructure. While shaking damage dominates the overall impact profile, our findings reveal that landslides substantially increase the spatial extent of risk, particularly in steep and remote regions where landslide impacts on buildings and roads can exceed $\text{50\%}$ of the number of buildings damaged by shaking. The results show a strong and statistically significant correlation between district remoteness and the proportion of road segments damaged by landslides (Spearman $\rho = 0.81$), which could compound the challenges of emergency response and of access to health care and government facilities. These findings are consistent with an urban-rural risk divide shaped by Nepal's diverse topography. This multi-hazard ensemble approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of earthquake risk than shaking-only assessments, offering valuable insights for contingency planning by humanitarian organisations and the Government of Nepal.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Earthquakes have killed more than two million people since 1900 \citep{England2011,Daniell2017} and remain one of the deadliest global hazards. Humanitarian contingency planning for future earthquakes, which comprises both preparation for earthquake response as well as pre-emptive efforts to reduce the impacts from a future earthquake \citep{IASC2007,IFRC2012}, is most likely to be effective if it is based on the best available scientific information on the associated risks \citep{Alexander2007}. This information should ideally include estimates of the scale of impacts in a future earthquake as well as the locations where those impacts are most likely to occur \citep{IASC2007}.

In contingency planning, the emphasis is typically placed exclusively on either worst-case or `maximum credible events' \citep[e.g.,][]{klugel_seismic_2008}. It can be useful, however, to distinguish between a range of possible or plausible impacts, especially when planning involves the allocation of often scarce resources. Existing approaches to seismic risk analysis typically fall into one of two categories \citep{Robinson2018, mcguire_deterministic_2001}. Deterministic approaches use scenarios that define the effects of one or a small number of possible future earthquakes \citep{Davies2015}, often chosen as the maximum credible event or the worst-case event that could be expected on a particular fault. In contrast, probabilistic approaches rely on estimates of the likelihood of exceeding a given value of ground motion from all possible seismic sources over a specified period of time, with the corresponding impacts from that level of shaking representing the total cumulative losses over the time period.

Building upon a body of work that has critically reviewed both probabilistic and deterministic approaches \citep{Bommer2002,Wang2003,Wang2011,Bilham2013, klugel_seismic_2008}, Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018} noted that, for many of the world's earthquake-prone areas, there are insufficient data on past earthquakes and their impacts to reliably apply either method. Borrowing from common practice in climate modelling, they proposed an alternative approach that used an ensemble of earthquake scenarios to identify patterns of emergent impacts. This allowed them to distinguish between consequences that were common to multiple scenarios -- and thus more likely to occur in future -- from those that were highly specific to a single future event \citep{Robinson2018}. They applied this ensemble approach to evaluate relative earthquake risk in Nepal to support contingency planning by the humanitarian clusters of the Humanitarian Country Team and the Government of Nepal. Importantly, because the different scenarios were equally weighted in the ensemble, they focused exclusively on relative risk between scenario outcomes. This approach forms the basis of the current earthquake emergency response and preparedness plan \citep{un_nepal2022earthquake}.

An important shortcoming of the scenario ensemble developed by Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018}, however, is that it only considered damage to buildings resulting from earthquake shaking. Experience in recent large continental earthquakes shows that shaking damage is only one part of a common chain of earthquake-related hazards \citep{Gill2014}. Landslides, debris flows, sediment aggradation, and subsequent flooding can have persistent impacts for years or decades after the earthquake \citep{Fan2019,Kincey2021, francis_fate_2022}, and earthquakes that trigger landslides typically have larger impacts than those that do not \citep{Budimir2014}. Planning for future earthquake impacts in continental settings should therefore account for this range of earthquake-related hazards.

Promisingly, multi-hazard approaches to risk assessment are now becoming more widespread (see recent reviews by \cite{Gill2022,Ward2022}). Multi-hazards are defined by UNDRR (\url{https://www.undrr.org/terminology/hazard}) as "events may occur simultaneously, cascadingly or cumulatively over time, and taking into account the potential interrelated effects". While many "multi-hazard" risk models overlay single hazards without considering their interactions \citep{Gill2014}, there is growing recognition that true multi-hazard risk assessment requires models that can consider interactions between different hazards and can capture spatial clustering or amplification effects \citep{Mignan2014,Tilloy2019,Dunant2021,Ming2022,Hochrainer-Stigler2023}.

Dunant et al. \citep{Dunant2025} proposed a novel multi-hazard impact model that used a hypergraph framework to link elements at risk -- such as buildings or roads -- within spatial domains that correspond to the areas exposed to a hazard trigger such as an earthquake. The hypergraph approach allowed efficient estimation of the impacts from cascading multi-hazards; in turn, this efficiency meant that the impacts of a scenario earthquake could be simulated repeatedly within a Monte Carlo framework to derive statistical estimations of the areas most likely to be affected. They evaluated the model against the 2015 Mw 7.8 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, and showed that the model could reasonably approximate the spatial patterns of building damage due to shaking as well as building and road damage due to earthquake-triggered landslides.

Here, we apply the multi-hazard impact model of Dunant et al. \citep{Dunant2025} to the scenario ensemble analysed by Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018}, in order to provide the first quantitative estimates of a suite of potential multi-hazard impacts arising from the next earthquake to occur in Nepal. This provides a substantially more complete picture of both the number and spatial distribution of earthquake-related impacts, including those arising from cascading hazards such as earthquake-triggered landslides, than was previously possible. Our purpose is not to predict the specific details of a future earthquake. Instead, we aim to illustrate the range of potential impacts that could arise from a suite of plausible earthquake scenarios, and the areas most likely to be affected by earthquake shaking and earthquake-triggered landslides, in order to support contingency planning for a future event by the Humanitarian Country Team and the Government of Nepal \citep{Sijapati2024}.

\section{Methods}
We first provide a brief description of the hypergraph multi-hazard impact model. We then describe the earthquake scenario ensemble that we use to drive the model, the experimental setup, the analysis of the ensemble outcomes, and the multi-hazard risk scores.

\subsection{Model description}

A full description of the hyperedge multi-hazard impact model was provided by Dunant et al. \citep{Dunant2025}, along with details of its application to the 2015 Mw 7.8 Gorkha earthquake. Here, we give an overview of the model and its key assumptions. The model operates on a network of nodes which represent the elements in the model -- in this case, hillslopes (represented by individual slope units), buildings, and roads. These nodes are connected by hyperedges, which can be thought of as polygons that enclose one or more nodes. Unlike edges in graph-based models, which link a single pair of nodes \citep{Dunant2021}, hyperedges can connect multiple nodes. For example, in the model used here a single hyperedge links all slope units within Nepal. Those slope units, in turn, can be represented by individual hyperedges that link all of the buildings and roads on each hillslope. In this way, we can define a nested set of hyperedges that preserve both the characteristics of each element -- for example, the topography of each slope unit, or the building type, or the susceptibilities to landslides or debris flows at each building location -- and the spatial relationships between those elements. The efficiency of the hypergraph network means that hundreds or thousands of separate simulations can be run for each earthquake scenario, with each simulation exploring a different pathway through the network of possible cascading impacts \citep{Dunant2025}. A Monte Carlo approach allows us to represent the uncertainty within a single scenario by generating a statistical distribution of possible outcomes \citep{dunant2021multihazards}, from which we derive the likelihood that each element is affected. By applying this process to each earthquake in an ensemble of plausible scenarios, we also capture inter-scenario variability. In this study, we summarise the within-scenario distribution by the expected number of damaged elements, so the variability we report reflects differences in impacts between possible future earthquakes; the within-scenario spread of outcomes is not propagated into the results presented here.

The implementation of the model described here is focused on the chain of hazards associated with large continental earthquakes. Earthquake shaking is assumed to cause direct damage to buildings via a set of fragility functions that relate peak ground acceleration (PGA) to the probability of reducing the building to a complete damage state (Figure S1). Shaking can also trigger landslides within individual slope units that fall within the footprint of the earthquake, and those landslides in turn may damage buildings or road segments within the slope unit. Because our focus is on the direct impacts of our chosen scenario earthquakes, we do not consider longer term or delayed hazards such as landslide reactivation by subsequent rainfall \citep{Fan2019,Kincey2021}, or consequent downslope changes in sediment storage and flood capacity. Thus, our results should be interpreted as reflecting the instantaneous coseismic effects that could be observed on the day of the earthquake. Other hazards, such as those triggered directly by rainfall, are also not considered, although they could be added via additional sets of hyperedges and interactions.

To generate a single earthquake impact scenario, we apply the expected ground shaking from our chosen earthquake to the hyperedge model and evaluate 10,000 separate outcomes from that event -- that is, we repeat the hyperedge model 10,000 times, each time following a separate path through the hyperedge network. This generates a distribution of model outcomes in terms of the likelihood that any building will be damaged by earthquake shaking or by landsliding, and the likelihood that any 100 m road segment will be damaged by landsliding. Those likelihoods can then be aggregated by administrative unit to identify the areas most likely to be affected by one or another of those components in the earthquake hazard chain. We then repeat this process for the other scenario earthquakes, and examine the ensemble of outcomes that result.

\subsection{Earthquake ensemble}

Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018} assembled an ensemble of 30 large ($M_w > 7$) earthquakes that could plausibly cause damage in Nepal, based on a combination of historical records and palaeoseismic evidence along with the distribution of major faults (Figure~\ref{fig:study}). These included earthquakes along the Main Himalayan Thrust, ranging in magnitude from $M_w$ 7.3 to 8.6, as well as earthquakes in the upper plate of the Main Himalayan Detachment on the Karakorum Fault ($M_w$ 7.8), the northern ($M_w$ 7.8) and southern ($M_w$ 7.3) parts of the Western Fault System, and the Thakkhola Graben ($M_w$ 7.3). For direct comparison of our modelling with their results, we adopt the same 30 scenario earthquakes. The pattern of PGA for each of these 30 events was modelled with OpenSHA \citep{Field2003}, using the ground motion prediction equations of Abrahamson and Silva \citep{Abrahamson2008}, exceedance probabilities of $\text{50\%}$, and shallow shear wave velocity (Vs30) derived from topographic slope \citep{Allen2009}. Maps of PGA for each of the 30 scenario earthquakes are shown in Figure \ref{fig:S2}.

\subsection{Experimental setup and data sources}

For each scenario earthquake, the model proceeds through the hyperedges that connect earthquake shaking to other elements in the model, checking to see whether each hyperedge is 'activated' -- that is, whether a building is damaged by shaking or a slope unit is affected by one or more landslides. If that hyperedge is activated, then the simulation propagates along that hyperedge and can cascade to other hyperedges through the network.

We first check the buildings within the footprint of the earthquake (defined by a PGA cutoff value of $0.1\,\text{g}$, below which the probability of complete damage is low for all building types; see Figure \ref{fig:S1}). We use building locations taken from the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team available from \url{https://data.humdata.org/dataset/hotosm_npl_buildings}, comprising approximately 7.1 million building polygons. Road locations are taken from the same source (\url{https://data.humdata.org/dataset/hotosm_npl_roads}; last accessed 1 January 2021) and divided into $100\,\text{m}$ segments, giving approximately 3.2 million road segments \citep{Dunant2025}. Construction types are not available for individual building polygons, so we use the best-available national-scale exposure data from the Modelling Exposure Through Earth Observation Routines (METEOR) project, available from \url{https://maps.meteor-project.org/map/building-exposure-map-of-nepal}. This dataset provides the relative proportions of different building types within each $90 \times 90\,\text{m}$ grid cell. For each building type, we define three separate fragility functions -- a `Low', `Mid', and `High' case -- representing the uncertainty in its structural performance (Figure \ref{fig:S1}). We then generate a single, representative fragility function for each grid cell by calculating a weighted average of these functions, where the weights are determined by the proportions of each building type in that cell. We then compare the weighted-average fragility function in each cell to the PGA value at the cell centroid to determine the likelihood of complete damage for each building $[0,1]$ for the low-, mid- and high-fragility cases. Dunant et al. \citep{Dunant2025} showed that the mid-fragility case was best able to reproduce observed building damage patterns and numbers in the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, so for simplicity we focus on the results with the mid-fragility case below.

For each slope unit, we compute the mean PGA value and use it to determine the probability of landsliding based on a lognormal function that is calibrated by observations in the 2015 Gorkha earthquake \citep{Dunant2025}. We then compare this probability against a uniform random deviate to decide whether a slope unit is activated during a simulation. Over multiple simulations, slopes that were more likely to slide will be activated more frequently. However, due to the probabilistic nature of this approach, the exact pattern of activations can vary between simulations. For each slope unit that is activated, the model proceeds to determine whether landslides are triggered and, if so, whether they impact buildings or road segments within that unit. To do so, we evaluate landslide susceptibility using a model that relies exclusively on static topographic variables (i.e., no earthquake-specific information). This model incorporates seven key factors: elevation, hillslope aspect, proximity to rivers, plan-view curvature, regional relief, local slope gradient, and a terrain ruggedness index. These factors are derived from a $10\,\text{m}$ resolution digital elevation model (DEM), which was created by downsampling the $5\,\text{m}$ DEM provided by the Advanced Land Observing Satellite World 3D (\url{https://www.aw3d.jp/en/products/standard/}). We applied the XGBoost machine learning algorithm, implemented in Python, to develop the susceptibility model \citep{Dunant2025}. For the purposes of this study, the model was trained using data from coseismic landslides triggered by the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, as documented by Kincey et al. \citep{Kincey2021}. The model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of $0.75$. It should be emphasised that this susceptibility model is optimised for the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, and it would be possible to use other susceptibility models within the hyperedge framework as required.

Whether or not a landslide is triggered in the slope unit is determined by drawing a value from a Gaussian distribution of landslide susceptibility with the same mean and standard deviation as the distribution of susceptibility values in that slope unit, and comparing that value with a uniform random deviate \citep{Dunant2025}. If the susceptibility value is smaller than the random deviate, then no landslide occurs and propagation along that hyperedge stops. If a landslide is triggered in a given slope unit, we perform a secondary check to assess whether individual buildings and road segments within that unit are impacted. This is done by comparing the landslide susceptibility value at the location of each asset (representing potential impact probability) with a uniform random deviate. If the deviate exceeds the susceptibility value, the asset is considered unaffected; otherwise, we assume it is impacted by the landslide and track the event. While the slope-unit-scale susceptibility governs the triggering of landslides, the local susceptibility value at each asset location is used as a proxy for spatially-variable impact likelihood. Because the susceptibility model is trained on the full footprint of coseismic landslides \citep{Kincey2021}, this impact likelihood encompasses both source and runout areas. This process is repeated iteratively across all activated slope units to generate a cascading impact scenario, simulating the spread of landslides and their effects on infrastructure throughout the region. We continue this process iteratively to search through all slope units in the network to generate a single outcome for that earthquake. This is repeated 10,000 times for each scenario earthquake. This results in a shaking impact likelihood and a landslide impact likelihood, both in the range $[0,1]$, for each building, and a landslide impact likelihood in the range $[0,1]$ for each $100\,\text{m}$ road segment in our national-scale dataset. Finally, we move on to the next earthquake in the 30-member ensemble and repeat the steps above.

\subsection{Analysis of ensemble outcomes}

Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018} focused on scenario earthquake impacts in terms of fatalities, using statistical relationships between building damage states, building occupation rates, and fatality rates. Because occupation rates vary over time (e.g., between day and night), they also considered three possible occurrence times for each of the 30 scenario earthquakes. Here, we focus instead on earthquake impacts on (static) buildings and roads, both through shaking and earthquake-triggered landsliding, and so we limit our analysis to the 30 earthquakes without considering occurrence time. Extending our multi-hazard results to estimate fatalities would require well-established empirical functions to relate landslide occurrence to fatality rates for both people in buildings and those using the road network \citep{VanWykDeVries2024}. These do not yet exist, although there is some recent research toward that goal \citep{Pollock2020,Luo2023,Hu2024}. Our choice to focus on the impacts to infrastructure is justified also by the common need in humanitarian contingency planning to focus on the numbers of affected households who will potentially require assistance \citep{IASC2007}. The detailed relationship between the locations of infrastructure, which are typically known, and the locations of people in the landscape over time, which are typically not known, remains an outstanding gap, and we return to this point in the discussion.

While the hypergraph model generates an impact likelihood for each building and each $100\,\text{m}$ road segment in our infrastructure database, it is important to remember that this is only valid in a probabilistic sense and does not represent a statement that `building X will be damaged in earthquake N'. \cite{Dunant2025} showed that the performance of the hypergraph model in the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, in terms of its ability to reproduce observed patterns of damage, improved as the model results were aggregated over larger and larger administrative areas. They identified the district level ($n = 77$ in Nepal) as providing the optimum trade-off between model performance and granularity of results. Despite not featuring in Nepal's federal structure after the adoption of the new constitution in 2015, district governments remain important for disaster management \citep{Bhandari2020}. Accordingly, we primarily aggregate our results at district level in the sections below. We do this by calculating the expected number of (1) buildings damaged by earthquake shaking, (2) buildings damaged by landsliding, and (3) road segments damaged by landsliding, taking each as the sum of the per-building (or per road segment) likelihood values in that district. For example, if a district contains 10 buildings and the mean per-building likelihood of damage by shaking is $0.5$, then on average we would expect 5 buildings to be completely damaged in that earthquake. Because the expected value of a sum is the sum of the individual expected values, these district totals do not require damage to be independent between buildings or between road segments. Spatial correlation (e.g., through co-location on a large landslide mass) would, however, widen the distribution of outcomes around these expected values.

We use the expected numbers of buildings damaged by shaking, buildings damaged by landsliding, and road segments damaged by landsliding to estimate the exceedance probability of impacts across the 30 scenario earthquakes. Because the scenarios are equally weighted and have no associated recurrence rates, exceedance probability here refers to the fraction of the 30 equally weighted scenarios in which a given impact is equalled or exceeded, not to a probability of occurrence over time. This allows us to determine both the median and worst-case for each type of impact and within each of Nepal's 77 districts.

We also examine the variability of each of our three types of impact across the different scenarios. If all scenarios result in similar impacts within a district, then the potential impacts are likely to occur irrespective of the details of the next earthquake. Conversely, if the impacts vary greatly between the different scenarios, then there is high uncertainty about what could occur in the next event. To capture this, we calculate the standard deviation of damage across the 30 ensemble members for each district. A high standard deviation indicates that the number of damaged assets depends strongly on which earthquake occurs, whereas a low standard deviation suggests a more consistent impact profile regardless of the specific event. Because it is expressed in numbers of assets, the standard deviation also scales with the number of exposed buildings or road segments; it therefore describes the absolute spread of damage that contingency plans may need to accommodate, rather than a relative measure of variability. For comparison, we also map the coefficient of variation (the standard deviation divided by the mean) across the 30 scenarios (Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}B, D, F), which does not depend on the number of exposed assets and indicates how variable impacts are relative to their typical level.

\subsection{Multi-hazard risk scores}

To extend our analysis to multi-hazard risk and identify districts where risk is specifically related to one hazard type or where risks from different hazards compound, we develop two multi-hazard risk scores. Both scores incorporate a remoteness factor, which serves as a proxy for the logistical challenges of post-disaster aid delivery. As in the work presented in \cite{Robinson2018}, measures of remoteness are an important component of relative risk because they attempt to account for the time it will take for communities to receive aid after a large earthquake.

We use remoteness index values estimated by \cite{banick2019}, who quantified physical remoteness within Nepal using a geographic information system-based cost-time model that calculated travel times from any location to essential services. They integrated digital elevation data, road networks, land cover, and seasonal conditions to model realistic travel impedance across varied terrain, using a $30\,\text{m}$ resolution raster grid across all of Nepal. Their model accounted for topographic constraints by implementing switchback algorithms for steep slopes, assigned different speeds to four hierarchical road classes, and incorporated seven land cover categories for off-road travel. Seasonal accessibility variations were captured through separate dry and monsoon season models, with appropriate speed reductions applied to different road types during adverse conditions. The resulting travel time estimates were validated by \cite{banick2019} against household survey data and expert consultations, then aggregated by administrative units to produce separate remoteness metrics for the dry and monsoon seasons. Their results specify the fraction of the population in each administrative unit for whom essential services can be accessed within one of eight time intervals ($0$--$30$ minutes, $30$ minutes--$1$ hour, $1$--$2$ hours, $2$--$4$ hours, $4$--$8$ hours, $8$--$16$ hours, $16$--$32$ hours, and $>32$ hours).

For our analysis, we focus on travel time to two different types of infrastructure: district headquarters, representing access to government support, and health facilities, both essential facilities in disaster response \citep{NationalPlanningCommission2015}. We weight the travel time intervals with a progressive weighting scale where weights increase exponentially with travel time, ranging from $0.0069$ for areas within 30 minutes of facilities to $1$ for areas more than 32 hours away ($w_i = 0.0069$, $0.0208$, $0.0417$, $0.0833$, $0.1667$, $0.3333$, $0.6667$ and $1$ for the eight intervals listed above, in order of increasing travel time). These weights correspond to the lower bound of each interval expressed as a fraction of one day (e.g., $1\,\text{h}/24\,\text{h} \approx 0.0417$), with 10 minutes assumed for the first interval and the weight of the open-ended final interval capped at 1. These weights are then multiplied by the fraction of the district population in each travel time interval, obtained by summing the population in that interval over the district's municipalities, to calculate a weighted remoteness value for each district. This weighting approach ensures that districts with larger populations facing longer travel times receive appropriately higher remoteness scores. So, for each district, the remoteness index $\text{RI}$, for either access to district headquarters or health facilities, is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:remoteness-index}
\text{RI} = \sum_{i} \left( w_{i} \times P_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

where $w_{i}$ represents the weight for travel time interval $i$, and $P_{i}$ represents the fraction of the district's population falling within that travel time interval. The resulting indices are normalised to [0,1] by dividing by the highest district value. The remoteness data of \cite{banick2019} use Nepal's pre-2015 administrative structure of 75 districts. Following the 2015 restructuring, Nawalparasi was split into Nawalpur and Parasi, and Rukum into Rukum East and Rukum West. Remoteness indices for these four new districts are inherited from their respective parent districts. The two indices were not combined, the risk scores defined below use only the dry-season remoteness index for access to district headquarters, as a proxy for access to government-led relief, while the health-facility index is used for comparison only (Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}).

To categorise districts by remoteness level, we employ a data-driven statistical approach rather than arbitrary thresholds, using K-means clustering of the district remoteness index. The silhouette score, which measures how well-defined and separated the clusters are, is calculated for cluster numbers ranging from 2 to 9 (Figure~\ref{fig:S4}). The silhouette score is highest for two clusters (0.76) but remains high for three (0.63); we retain three groups, corresponding to low, medium, and high remoteness ($n = 55$, 14 and 8 districts, respectively), because they are easier to interpret for contingency planning. Finally, the risk scores combine the worst-case impact values (buildings and roads damaged) with the remoteness index as weighted sums (Equations~\ref{eq:absolute-risk-score} and \ref{eq:normalized-risk-score}).

\subsubsection{Absolute risk score}

This index is designed to identify hotspots of large-scale earthquake impacts. The score combines remoteness with absolute worst-case damage using a weighted sum approach. The formula is:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:absolute-risk-score}
\text{Risk Score}_{\text{Absolute}} = 0.5 \times R + 0.5 \times \frac{S + L}{\max(S + L)}
\end{equation}

where $R$ is the normalised remoteness index, $S$ is the absolute worst-case shaking damage in a district (in terms of the number of buildings affected), and $L$ is the absolute worst-case landslide damage (in terms of the number of buildings and road segments combined). Because the worst cases of the different impact types can arise in different scenarios, $(S + L)$ is a component-wise worst case, that is, an upper bound on the worst single-scenario total; in practice, it exceeds the largest single-scenario total by less than 1\% in every district. Each damaged building and each damaged $100\,\text{m}$ road segment counts as one impact, and a building damaged by both shaking and landsliding contributes to both $S$ and $L$. The total impact $(S + L)$ is normalised by dividing by its maximum value across all 77 districts, so that the most-impacted district has a value of 1, before being combined with remoteness in a weighted sum. This approach treats remoteness and earthquake impacts as two independent but equally important components (0.5 weight each). Districts score highly if they have either high remoteness or high total impact, or both. This score helps identify areas that are likely to require a substantial humanitarian response.

\subsubsection{Normalised risk score}

This index identifies districts where the community is most severely threatened, regardless of its size. It combines remoteness with proportional worst-case damage. The calculation is:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalized-risk-score}
\text{Risk Score}_{\text{Normalised}} = 0.5 \times R + 0.25 \times \frac{S_{\text{prop}}}{\max(S_{\text{prop}})} + 0.25 \times \frac{L_{\text{prop}}}{\max(L_{\text{prop}})}
\end{equation}

where $R$ is the normalised remoteness index, $S_{\text{prop}}$ is the proportional worst-case shaking damage (in terms of the percentage of buildings affected), and $L_{\text{prop}}$ is the proportional worst-case landslide damage (the sum of the percentages of buildings and of road segments damaged). Each hazard type is normalised independently, by dividing by its maximum value across all 77 districts, before being combined. This weighted sum approach (50\% remoteness, 25\% shaking, 25\% landslides) highlights small, remote districts that are anticipated to suffer impacts to large proportions of their infrastructure, irrespective of the population or the extent of development. The district rankings produced by both scores are robust to moderate changes in the weights ($\rho \geq 0.97$), although the ten highest-ranked districts under the absolute score depend more on the remoteness weight (Table~\ref{tab:S1}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Multi-hazard impacts}

The total modelled number of buildings damaged by shaking varies between the different earthquakes in the ensemble, from approximately 8,600 for a Mw 7.8 Karakoram earthquake to approximately 3,400,000 for a Mw 8.6 in the Midwest, West, and Central regions of Nepal. For comparison, the Mw 7.8 Gorkha earthquake in central Nepal caused the destruction or damage of 981,000 buildings \citep{NationalPlanningCommission2015}. In contrast, the number of modelled building impacts by landsliding varies from approximately 80 for the Karakorum earthquake to approximately 178,000 for the Mw 8.6 event, and the number of modelled 100 m road segments impacted by landsliding varies from just over 100 to approximately 150,000 for the same earthquakes. For any given earthquake within the ensemble, each type of landslide impact is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than shaking damage.

Exceedance probability curves (Figure~\ref{fig:exceedance}) indicate that median national-level impacts include approximately 640,000 buildings damaged by shaking, 32,000 buildings damaged by landslides, and 29,000 road segments damaged by landslides. These distributions are right-skewed, with fewer high-damage scenarios reflecting the fact that large impacts occur in only a subset of the modelled events.

Both the total number of modelled earthquake impacts and the ratio of damage by landslides to damage by shaking vary strongly across Nepal, depending upon both the shaking patterns in the scenario earthquakes and the underlying susceptibility to landsliding. Thus, while building damage from earthquake shaking represents the largest single category of damage in all of Nepal’s 77 districts, its proportion of the total number of impacts is highly variable (Figure~\ref{fig:district_ratio}). If we consider the worst case of each impact type in each district, landslide impacts on buildings and roads make up between 1\% and 84\% of the number of buildings damaged by shaking. The ratio is lowest for districts located in the low-elevation, low-relief Terai region in southern Nepal; these areas are densely populated, with large numbers of buildings, but have low landslide susceptibility \citep{Kincey2021, Kincey2024}. Conversely, the highest ratios are found in the sparsely-populated mountain districts along the northern border of Nepal, including Darchula, Manang, Dolpa, and Mustang (see Figure 1 for locations). This result highlights the importance of topography and local hazard susceptibility in shaping damage patterns.

Exceedance plots by district show that between 33\% and 63\% of scenarios result in an expected number of at least one building per district completely damaged by shaking (Figure~\ref{fig:district_exceedance}A). In comparison, 30\% to 87\% of scenarios result in an expected number of at least one building damaged by landslides (Figure~\ref{fig:district_exceedance}B), and 27\% to 77\% in an expected number of at least one road segment damaged by landslides (Figure~\ref{fig:district_exceedance}C). However, the median number of landslide-induced impacts is generally below 10 per district, confirming that landslide impacts are smaller in number than shaking impacts but still widespread across scenarios. As expected, the exceedance probability patterns show distinct physiographic variations \citep{upreti_physiography_2001}. Districts in the Terai lowlands in southern Nepal have high exceedance probabilities for shaking damage but consistently low probabilities for landslide impacts, reflecting their large building exposure but low relief and limited slope instability. In contrast, hill and middle mountain districts have more variable exceedance patterns, with several districts showing higher landslide exceedance probabilities than shaking probabilities, particularly for road damage. High mountain districts have the most variable patterns, with some showing very low exceedance probabilities due to sparse infrastructure, while others have disproportionately high landslide exceedance probabilities relative to their larger shaking exposure. These physiographic trends highlight how topographic setting fundamentally controls the relative importance of different hazard components across Nepal's diverse landscapes.

Districts with the largest expected numbers of damaged buildings, both in the median and the worst-case scenarios, are concentrated in the more populated southern and central areas of the country (Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}A), highlighting not only building density but also patterns of infrastructure vulnerability (Figure\ref{fig:S3}). Districts in the more remote and more sparsely-populated northwest have relatively low total numbers of shaking-damaged buildings, even in the worst case. In contrast, while the absolute numbers of expected landslide impacts are lower, the largest number of landslide impacts are concentrated in the hill and mountain districts across Nepal (Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}C, E). Road impacts from landslides are widely distributed through the hill and mountain districts, with particular hotspots in Sindhupalchok, Baglung, Rolpa, Bajura, and Bajhang districts (Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}E). These areas should be considered at the highest risk of disruption to the road network after a future large earthquake. Building impacts from landslides are more focused on the hill and middle mountain districts, likely because of the concentration of population in those areas (Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}C). Not surprisingly, expected landslide impacts are lower in the flat-lying southern districts of the Terai, along the border with India (Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}D, F). The dominance of shaking impacts over landslide impacts means that their sum, which we take as the worst-case expected total number of earthquake impacts (the sum of the worst cases of each impact type), mirrors the distribution of shaking damage, although numbers of impacts are notably higher in the hill districts in particular (compare Figure~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps}C and A).

\subsection{Remoteness, impacts, and risk scores}

The index of remoteness from health facilities (Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}A) shows values ranging from near 0 in southern districts to 1 in Manang, followed by Dolpa (0.65); other northern and northwestern districts, such as Humla, Darchula and Kalikot, have values of 0.3--0.4. Comparing remoteness from health facilities with remoteness from the district headquarters (Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}B) shows that remoteness values from district headquarters are higher than those from health facilities in 91\% of districts. The largest differences are found in Darchula, Bajhang, Dolpa, Jajarkot and Humla, while Manang is the only district that lies clearly above the 1:1 line.

The relationship between remoteness and road network damage is further detailed in the bottom panels of Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}, which show that districts categorised as high remoteness experience a significantly larger proportion of road network damage (median $\approx 20\%$) than low remoteness districts (median $\approx 9\%$). Across all districts, remoteness is strongly correlated with the proportion of road segments damaged by landslides (Spearman $\rho = 0.81$). Conversely, Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}D shows that in terms of absolute numbers, low remoteness districts (often more urbanized) bear the highest total impact count (in terms of shaking and landslide damage combined).

While Figure~\ref{fig:risk_score}A maps the absolute score to identify regions facing the highest total burden of earthquake-related impacts, Panel B shows the effect of applying a normalisation approach to account for local vulnerabilities, thereby highlighting communities subjected to the most acute risk, regardless of absolute numbers.

Figure \ref{fig:risk_score} illustrates how different risk metrics reveal distinct spatial patterns of earthquake risk across Nepal. The absolute risk score (Figure 8A) combines remoteness with total worst-case damage (buildings and roads). This metric identifies districts facing the largest aggregate disaster burden, with elevated scores scattered around Nepal, in districts such as Dolpa, Kathmandu, Bajhang, Chitawan, Dang and Morang. These areas score highly due to either substantial absolute infrastructure exposure, high remoteness, or both factors combined. In contrast, the normalised risk score (Figure 8B) accounts for proportional damage relative to each district's infrastructure and population size. This normalisation fundamentally shifts the risk geography, revealing acute vulnerabilities in hill and mountain districts, especially in the north and northwest of the country, where communities face disproportionately high percentages of infrastructure loss in combination with high remoteness. 

\section{Discussion}

Our hypergraph model extends the earthquake scenario ensemble approach developed by Robinson et al. (2018) to include both primary hazards from earthquake shaking as well as secondary hazards from coseismic landslides. In a tectonic setting like Nepal, characterised by highly variable topography and a well-established chain of earthquake-related hazards \citep{Kincey2021}, this allows us to develop a fuller picture of the potential impacts in those future scenario earthquakes. We consider some of the key differences between the scenario ensemble results of Robinson et al. (2018) and our findings and their implications for humanitarian contingency planning, as well as some of the important limitations of our approach.

\subsection{Differences between shaking-only and multi-hazard scenario outcomes}

Robinson et al. (2018) derived a normalised risk index based on worst-case earthquake-shaking impacts across their ensemble, combined with remoteness and a vulnerability index, and used that to rank Nepal's 75 pre-2015 districts (Figure~\ref{fig:robinson_comparison}A). It is useful to compare that shaking-only risk estimate with our normalised risk score shown in Figure~\ref{fig:risk_score}B, to assess whether the districts prioritised in contingency planning would change. Direct comparison of the district-level rankings between the two risk models yields a Spearman rank correlation coefficient of 0.80 (Figure~\ref{fig:robinson_comparison}B), so the relative risk profile of Nepal's districts is broadly preserved at the national scale. This result is expected, because the two analyses share the same 30-member earthquake ensemble and shaking damage dominates total impact counts in the large majority of districts. However, the overall correlation conceals meaningful divergence in specific districts (Figure~\ref{fig:robinson_comparison}C), particularly mountain districts in northern Nepal such as Manang, Rasuwa and Mustang, which rank 39--59 places higher in our score than in that of Robinson et al. (2018). Because the two scores differ in their hazard model, remoteness indicator and vulnerability components as well as in the inclusion of landslides, these differences cannot be attributed to landslides alone. Indeed, a shaking-only version of our normalised score (Equation~\ref{eq:normalized-risk-score} without the landslide term) has the same correlation with the score of Robinson et al. (2018) ($\rho = 0.80$), indicating that most of the divergence reflects the different remoteness and vulnerability measures. To isolate the contribution of landslides, we therefore compare our normalised score with and without the landslide term. The two rankings are highly correlated ($\rho = 0.97$), but including landslides moves several hill and mountain districts substantially up the ranking, most notably Rasuwa (22 places), Baglung and Myagdi (12 places each), and Gulmi (8 places), while districts with little landslide damage relative to shaking move down (Table~\ref{tab:S2}). These are the districts in which the multi-hazard approach adds most to a shaking-only assessment, consistent with the documented tendency of shaking-only frameworks to underestimate risk in areas of high topographic relief where secondary hazards are prevalent \citep{Budimir2014}. This reinforces the need for multi-hazard approaches that account for the full chain of earthquake-related impacts in continental earthquake settings.

The spatial variability in the ratio of landslide to shaking damage (Figure~\ref{fig:district_ratio}) further highlights the role of local topography and landslide susceptibility in shaping district-level impact patterns. Districts in the low-relief Terai show consistently low landslide contributions, while northern mountain districts such as Darchula, Manang, and Dolpa show the highest ratios. This heterogeneity implies that risk thresholds derived from aggregated national or regional statistics will systematically misrepresent the relative severity of risk in topographically complex settings, and that locally-informed, multi-hazard assessments are necessary to support effective contingency planning across diverse physiographic regions like Nepal.

\subsection{Road network impacts and compounding effects of remoteness}

The quantification of landslide damage to the road network represents one of the most operationally significant contributions of this study relative to previous scenario ensemble approaches for Nepal. Road infrastructure is the primary means of access for emergency responders, medical and relief supplies, and humanitarian personnel in Nepal's hill and mountain districts, and its disruption following a large earthquake can substantially delay response times and compound the human consequences of the event \citep{Koks2019, NationalPlanningCommission2015}. Our results show that the proportion of the road network damaged by landslides is substantially higher in districts classified as highly remote (median $\approx 20\%$) than in those with low remoteness (median $\approx 9\%$), and this disparity is compounded by the fact that remote districts already face longer pre-event travel times to health facilities and district headquarters (Figure~\ref{fig:remoteness}). The two effects are likely to reinforce each other: the steep, high-relief terrain that elevates landslide exposure is also what makes post-event access most critical and most difficult to restore. Districts such as Dolpa, Bajhang, and Darchula illustrate this compound vulnerability most acutely, combining high remoteness with worst-case landslide damage to 17--29\% of their road segments.

These findings have direct implications for pre-event contingency planning. The identification of districts where road damage is both severe and consistent across multiple scenarios provides a basis for prioritising alternative access options, including pre-positioning of emergency supplies and response capacity \citep[e.g.,][]{Sijapati2024}, the development of secondary route alternatives, and planning for aerial transport logistics in the most inaccessible areas. In districts where extensive landslide damage to roads is likely in a large earthquake, post-event road access cannot be assumed, and pre-event preparation is correspondingly critical \citep{IASC2007}.

\subsection{Scale dependence of hazards}

The importance of secondary hazards such as landsliding, relative to primary earthquake shaking, varies substantially with spatial scale and underlying exposure patterns. In densely populated districts with large building stocks and low topographic relief, landslide impacts represent a small addition to shaking damage, sitting at the lower end of the 1--84\% range observed across all districts (Figure~\ref{fig:district_ratio}). However, in smaller, mountainous districts with steep terrain and limited infrastructure, landslide impacts on buildings and roads can reach 84\% of the number of buildings damaged by shaking (Figure~\ref{fig:district_ratio}). While landslides account for a small fraction of national-level damage -- with national totals for each type of landslide impact one to two orders of magnitude below shaking impacts (Figure~\ref{fig:exceedance}) -- they therefore have the potential to almost double the impacts in specific localities, transforming what would otherwise appear as a moderate shaking scenario into a much more damaging multi-hazard event. Planning frameworks that rely solely on national or regional statistics may therefore systematically underestimate risk in vulnerable localities where cascading hazards dominate.

\subsection{Urban--rural risk contrast and geographic disparities}

The results are consistent with a spatially structured urban--rural risk divide, with direct implications for disaster risk governance in Nepal. Urban and peri-urban districts in the Terai and major river valleys face the greatest absolute burden from earthquake shaking, a consequence of their high population density and building stock. However, the most severe proportional losses -- and likely the greatest challenges for post-event response -- are concentrated in remote rural districts of the hill and mountain areas. These districts face disproportionately high percentages of road network damage in combination with high remoteness from health facilities and government support (Figures~\ref{fig:worst_case_maps} and \ref{fig:remoteness}). Although we do not model recovery capacity directly, longstanding disparities in infrastructure investment and institutional capacity across Nepal's physiographic regions \citep{NationalPlanningCommission2014} suggest that the communities whose physical connectivity is most vulnerable to earthquake-triggered landslides may also be among those least equipped to absorb and recover from disaster impacts. The persistence of such geographic inequalities in disaster risk and response capacity has been widely documented across multi-hazard settings \citep{Wisner2004}.

Our results also show clear spatial differences in how much earthquake-related impacts vary between scenarios, as measured by the standard deviation across the 30 scenario earthquakes (Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}A, C, E). Low standard deviation in building damage from earthquake shaking (Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}A) is to be expected in western Nepal, simply because of the smaller number of buildings overall in those districts. For landslide impacts, however, the largest variability is found in hill and mountain districts of central and western Nepal, such as Sindhupalchok, Baglung, Bajhang, Rolpa and Jajarkot (Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}C, E), pointing to the potential for localised but highly disruptive impacts due to particular earthquakes. In these areas, contingency planning for median or `typical' earthquake impacts may thus obscure a real and important distinction between the majority of events in the ensemble, which have limited impact, and a few earthquakes that can have locally severe effects. The 2023 Mw 5.7 Jajarkot earthquake provides a potential illustration; despite its moderate size, it killed 154 people and damaged approximately 62,000 households, affecting about 250,000 people \citep{UNRCO2024}. In these highly variable areas, it is especially important to plan for the worst-case future event. Relative to their typical level, landslide impacts are instead most variable in Terai districts such as Banke, Bardiya, Siraha and Saptari (coefficient of variation up to 2.85; Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}D, F), where they are small and occur in only a few scenarios. The coefficient of variation therefore highlights where impacts are rare rather than where they are large, and the absolute spread shown in Figure~\ref{fig:standard_deviation}A, C, E is the more relevant measure for planning response capacity.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations are inherent to the ensemble approach. While the analysis captures large-scale impacts from high-magnitude earthquakes ($M_w > 7$), it does not include the effects of locally destructive moderate-magnitude events such as the 2023 Jajarkot earthquake. As mentioned above, these events can cause severe localised impacts and warrant dedicated modelling efforts. While they could be included in an enhanced scenario ensemble, the number of potential earthquake sources and locations increases dramatically as the minimum scenario magnitude decreases (Robinson et al., 2018). In addition, we focus here on physical infrastructure damage and do not explicitly model fatalities, as infrastructure impact alone is not sufficient for risk-to-life estimates from landslides \citep{VanWykDeVries2024}. Future work integrating dynamic exposure and population mobility data is expected to improve casualty estimation for both shaking and landslide hazards.

A further limitation concerns the transferability of the susceptibility and fragility models across the ensemble. Both the landslide susceptibility model and the building fragility functions were calibrated using observations from the 2015 $M_w$ 7.8 Gorkha earthquake, which involved thrust rupture on the Main Himalayan Thrust beneath central Nepal. Several of the 30 scenario earthquakes considered here involve different fault systems and fault slip orientations -- including the Karakoram Fault and the Thakkhola Graben -- for which no independent calibration data on earthquake-triggered landslides are currently available. The degree to which the susceptibility and fragility relationships derived from the Gorkha event can be generalised to these different tectonic and geomorphic settings is uncertain and represents an important topic for future investigation.

It is also important to note that the hyperedge model as described here accounts only for earthquake shaking and coseismic landslides. Thus, there is no consideration of either new post-seismic landsliding, or the reactivation and evolving spatial footprint of pre-seismic and coseismic landslides by post-earthquake rainfall \citep{Kincey2021,Kincey2023,Shrestha2026}. As a result, the modelled impacts shown here should be taken as an estimate of impacts that could be expected to occur on the day of a future earthquake, and are highly likely to be minima compared to the full time-integrated inventory of both coseismic and post-seismic impacts. Recent work by \citet{Harvey2026} on an extended 30-year multi-temporal inventory of landsliding in Nepal has shown that, at the scale of the entire country, the Gorkha earthquake produced only a small increase in the total coverage of landslides across Nepal. They also showed that, at any given time, c. 80 percent of landslide area in Nepal was visible in the preceding year, implying that most of that landslide area represents reactivated events rather than entirely new failures. Thus, our use of susceptibility - which by definition indicates the most likely areas of new landslide occurrence - underestimates the potential landslide footprint in a future earthquake. Future iterations of the hyperedge model could incorporate runout from existing landslides \citep[e.g.,][]{Kincey2023} as well as coseismic landsliding, in order to close this gap.

Finally, we report expected district-level impacts, which do not depend on whether damage to individual buildings and road segments is independent. In practice, co-location of multiple assets within large landslide source or runout areas introduces positive spatial correlation in outcomes, which widens the range of impacts that could occur within a single scenario, particularly in districts with large, contiguous slope units. Because we do not report this within-scenario spread, future work that characterises the full distribution of outcomes, including spatially correlated failures, would help to quantify this additional source of uncertainty.


\section{Conclusions}

This study extends scenario ensemble-based earthquake risk assessments by incorporating coseismic landslide hazards into the scenario framework developed by Robinson et al. \citep{Robinson2018}. We have leveraged the hyperedge method for cascading scenario generation developed by Dunant et al. \citep{Dunant2025} and applied it to a suite of 30 potential future earthquakes in Nepal. The resulting multi-hazard ensemble reveals that landslides substantially increase the spatial extent of risk, particularly in steep and remote regions of Nepal. Although landslides account for a small proportion of total building damage compared to shaking, their contribution to overall risk is substantial. In hill and mountain districts, worst-case landslide impacts on buildings and roads can exceed 50\% of the worst-case number of buildings damaged by shaking, indicating that landslide damage can be a critical additional component of earthquake damage under specific scenarios. This underscores the spatial heterogeneity in hazard impacts across the country.

The analysis also illustrates how landslides can significantly affect transport infrastructure, particularly in remote areas, which could reduce access to both government services and health care facilities and complicate emergency response. The strong correlation between remoteness and anticipated road damage highlights the importance of pre-event planning and infrastructure resilience, as emphasised in national frameworks such as the Earthquake Response Preparedness Plan (ERPP). The current ensemble does not account for moderate-magnitude (Mw 5-6) but locally destructive events, such as the 2023 Jajarkot earthquake. This points to the need for complementary localised modelling approaches to capture risk in under-represented areas.

Finally, the findings are consistent with a persistent urban--rural risk divide, shaped by geographic and infrastructural disparities. Addressing this divide remains a key priority for disaster risk reduction. Future improvements in fatality modelling and the integration of high-resolution mobility and exposure data will be essential for producing more accurate and actionable multi-hazard risk assessments.
